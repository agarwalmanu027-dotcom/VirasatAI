Ajanta & Ellora Caves, Maharashtra — story content

Ajanta and Ellora are often named together, but they are two separate heritage sites in Maharashtra. Understanding their differences makes both more rewarding.

At Ajanta, Buddhist monasteries and worship halls were cut into a cliff above the Waghora River. Work took place in two main phases, beginning before the Common Era and returning several centuries later. Paintings, sculpture and architecture preserve a remarkable record of Buddhist art. Notice how a painted figure or carved shrine belongs to the room around it.

Ellora offers a different sequence. Its thirty-four principal caves include Buddhist, Hindu and Jain monuments. These religious groups developed their own spaces within a shared rocky landscape. The celebrated Kailasa temple, Cave Sixteen, demonstrates the extraordinary possibilities of cutting a temple from the rock.

At both sites, the makers removed material to create columns, halls and sacred images. Their work required careful planning before the finished space could emerge.

Both were recognised by UNESCO in nineteen eighty-three. They also require careful visiting: respect barriers, avoid touching painted and carved surfaces, and follow photography guidance.

Plan Ajanta and Ellora as distinct places, each deserving time. Together they reveal different chapters of faith, artistic ambition and the skill that transformed cliffs into places of worship and community life.
