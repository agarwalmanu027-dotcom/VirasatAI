Khajuraho Monuments, Madhya Pradesh — story content

Welcome to Khajuraho, where architecture and sculpture were designed to work together. These temples preserve the artistic achievement of the Chandela rulers, especially during the tenth and eleventh centuries. Hindu and Jain monuments share the wider heritage landscape.

The temples are arranged in western, eastern and southern groups. A closer look begins with their overall form. Raised platforms lift the buildings above their surroundings, while clustered towers lead the eye toward the principal spire above the sanctum. This is the northern Indian tradition known as Nagara architecture.

Then move your attention to the carved surfaces. Divine figures, musicians, dancers, teachers, domestic scenes and couples fill the stone with different aspects of sacred and social life. The famous intimate scenes are part of a much wider artistic programme.

A temple is also a sequence of spaces. Entrance porches, halls and the inner sanctum guide movement from the outside toward the sacred centre. Sculpture and architecture shape that journey together.

Recognised as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in nineteen eighty-six, Khajuraho rewards slow observation. Compare the large outline with a single small carving. The lasting achievement lies in the relationship between them, and in the skill that gave stone such an expressive human presence.
