At Apollo Bunder, Mumbai meets the Arabian Sea through one of its most familiar landmarks: the Gateway of India. The broad arch was built to commemorate the visit of King George the Fifth and Queen Mary in 1911. Designed by George Wittet and completed in 1924, it belongs to the period when Bombay was a major port under British rule.

Look beyond its famous outline. The central opening, dome and smaller architectural details draw on Indian and Islamic forms within an Indo-Saracenic design. Yellow basalt and concrete give the monument its substantial presence. Rising about twenty-six metres, it was intended to make an impressive statement at the waterfront.

That setting is part of its meaning. A gateway facing the sea speaks of arrivals, departures and the connection between the city and places beyond the horizon. The monument's original royal purpose belongs to colonial history, while its familiar image has become part of Mumbai's public identity.

Pause to compare the heavy stonework with the open expanse of water behind it. A photograph records the arch, but the wider scene explains why it matters here. The Gateway introduces a city shaped by maritime connections, changing political power and the many people who have made this shoreline their own.
